Open ten Moroccan supplier websites and you will read the same sentence ten times: 100% pure, organic, cold-pressed. Everyone says it, so it tells you nothing. The difference between a good supplier and an expensive mistake shows up somewhere else: in the documents they send, the questions they ask you, and how they behave when you ask for proof.

This guide is the checklist we walk new buyers through, whether they end up ordering from us or not.

## First, Be Clear About What You Are Buying

You cannot judge a supplier until you know your own specification. Buyers who skip this step end up comparing prices for products that are not the same.

### Cosmetic, deodorized or culinary?

- **Cosmetic argan oil** is pressed from raw, unroasted kernels. It is light gold with a mild nutty smell.
- **Deodorized cosmetic oil** has had most of that smell removed. Formulators who add fragrance often prefer it.
- **Culinary argan oil** comes from roasted kernels. It is darker and smells toasted.

These are three different products at three different prices. If one quote seems cheap, check first whether it is even the same grade.

### Organic or conventional?

Organic certification costs the producer real money: audits, segregated stock, paperwork. It only makes sense for you if you plan to put "organic" on your label. If you do not, conventional oil with a clean COA may serve you just as well.

### Volume and format

A 30 ml private label run and a 200-liter drum order are different businesses. Some suppliers are excellent at one and poor at the other, so ask directly which one they do most.

## Producer, Exporter or Trader? Find Out Who You Are Really Talking To

Morocco's argan trade has three kinds of sellers:

1. **Producers** press the oil themselves, or run a tight network of cooperatives.
2. **Exporters** buy from several cooperatives, then test, blend and ship.
3. **Traders** resell whatever they can source that week.

None of these is automatically bad. A good exporter with strong lab control can be more consistent than a small producer. What matters is that they tell you the truth about which one they are.

Ask three questions: Where exactly is the oil pressed? Can I visit, or see a live video walk-through? Will every batch I receive be traceable to its origin? A trader will usually get vague at the second question.

## How to Read an Argan Oil COA (the Step Most Buyers Skip)

A Certificate of Analysis is the single most useful document a supplier can give you, and the one most buyers file without reading. Here is what the numbers mean.

| Parameter | What it tells you | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| Acidity (free fatty acids) | Freshness and handling of the kernels and oil | Extra-virgin: below 0.8 g/100 g. Fine virgin: 0.8-1.5. Ordinary virgin: 1.5-2.5. Lampante: above 2.5 (Moroccan standard NM 08.5.090). |
| Peroxide value | Primary oxidation (early rancidity) | Low values for fresh, well-stored oil. Published studies of good argan oils report figures around 1-3 meq O2/kg. If it looks high, ask when the oil was pressed. |
| UV extinction (K232 / K270) | Oxidation products; also helps detect refining | Must fall within the NM 08.5.090 limits for the declared grade. |
| Fatty acid profile | Oil identity | Argan is an oleic-linoleic oil. A profile outside that pattern is a warning sign. |
| Sterols | Authenticity - the real purity test | Spinasterol and schottenol dominate; campesterol is present only at very low levels. Many cheaper oils contain much more campesterol, so dilution usually shows up here. |
| Tocopherols (vitamin E) | Nutritional / marketing value | Virgin argan oil typically contains roughly 600-900 mg/kg. |

### A COA without a batch number is just a brochure

Every COA should carry a batch number, an analysis date and the name of the lab. A generic "product specification" that never changes from order to order does not prove anything about the oil in your drum. Ask for the batch COA before shipment, not after the oil arrives.

## Certifications: Verify Them, Do Not Just Look at the Logo

Logos are easy to copy, and certificates expire. Before you trust one:

- **Check the certificate itself:** company name, certificate number, certifying body, scope (which products are covered) and expiry date.
- **Look it up.** Operations certified to the USDA organic program can be checked in the USDA Organic Integrity Database. Most European certifiers also let you verify certificates online.
- **Check the scope.** A certificate for argan oil does not cover the supplier's black soap or clay.
- **For organic imports into the EU,** each shipment needs its own electronic certificate of inspection in the EU TRACES system. Ask whether the supplier has issued these before.

**Be careful with "FDA approved" or "FDA certified".** The FDA does not approve or certify cosmetic products. Under the US MoCRA rules, cosmetic facilities register with the FDA and products are listed - that is not approval. A supplier who says "FDA approved" is either confused or hoping you are.

**ISO 22716 (cosmetic GMP)** is a meaningful sign that production follows hygiene and documentation rules. If you plan to sell finished private label products, it is worth asking for.

## The Sample Test That Actually Tells You Something

Everyone requests samples. Few buyers use them properly. Here is a protocol that works:

1. **Ask for the sample and its COA from the same batch.** A beautiful sample from "some batch" proves little.
2. **Compare blind.** Put samples from two or three suppliers in unlabeled bottles. Smell them, rub them on your skin, look at them in daylight.
3. **Send your favorite to an independent lab** if you are ordering in drums. A fatty acid and sterol panel costs far less than one bad batch.
4. **Keep part of every sample sealed.** When the bulk order arrives, you have a reference to compare against.

The most common complaint we hear from buyers is the bait-and-switch: an excellent sample, then a bulk shipment that looks and smells different. Step 4 is your protection. Put in writing that the bulk order must match the approved sample and the pre-shipment COA.

## Red Flags We Would Walk Away From

- A price far below what serious producers quote for the same grade.
- No batch COA, or the same PDF for every batch.
- "FDA approved" claims, or certificates that do not match the company name.
- No samples, or heavy sample fees that are never deducted from the first order.
- No clear answer about where the oil is pressed.
- Long-term storage in clear plastic - light damages the oil.
- Medical claims such as "cures eczema" or "heals hair loss". A supplier who is loose with claims is often loose with compliance too.
- Pressure to pay 100% upfront on a large first order.
- No questions about your market or your use.

The last one is subtle. A good supplier needs to know where you are selling so they can prepare the right documents. If they only want your money, they will not ask.

## Questions to Ask Before Your First Order

Copy these into your first email:

1. Is the oil cosmetic, deodorized or culinary? Is it virgin, and which acidity grade?
2. Where is it pressed, and how is each batch traced?
3. Can you send a batch COA with acidity, peroxide value, fatty acids and sterols?
4. Which certifications do you hold, and under which certificate numbers?
5. What are your MOQ, lead time and payment terms?
6. Which Incoterms do you offer: EXW, FOB or CIF?
7. What happens if the bulk order does not match the approved sample?
8. How long do you keep retention samples from each batch?

How a supplier answers tells you almost as much as what they answer. Fast, specific, documented answers are a good sign.

## Why the Cheapest Quote Is Rarely the Cheapest

Two quotes can look 2 euros apart per liter and end up 5 euros apart once the oil reaches your warehouse. Before comparing, bring every quote to the same basis:

- **Same grade and certification.**
- **Same Incoterm.** An EXW price leaves you to pay for everything after the supplier's door; a CIF price already includes sea freight and insurance.
- **Same packaging.**
- **Documents included.** Some suppliers charge extra for the certificate of origin or organic paperwork.

Argan prices also move with the harvest. Drought years reduce the kernel supply, and prices follow. A supplier who warns you about this before harvest season is looking after the relationship.

## How to Protect Yourself on the First Order

- **Put the specification in the pro-forma invoice:** grade, acidity limit, packaging, and "must match approved sample".
- **Ask for the pre-shipment batch COA** before paying the balance.
- **Use staged payment.** A deposit followed by the balance against documents is common and fair to both sides.
- **Start smaller than your forecast.** A first order at half your planned volume costs a little more per liter, but buys proof that the supplier delivers what they promised.
- **Keep everything in writing,** including WhatsApp confirmations of changes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I know if argan oil from a supplier is pure?

Ask for a batch COA that includes a fatty acid and sterol analysis. Argan oil has an unusual sterol profile with very little campesterol, so dilution with cheaper oils usually shows up there. For large orders, have a sample tested by an independent lab.

### What acidity should good argan oil have?

Under the Moroccan standard NM 08.5.090, extra-virgin argan oil has an acidity below 0.8 g/100 g. Fine virgin oil falls between 0.8 and 1.5.

### Is "FDA approved" argan oil a real thing?

No. The FDA does not approve cosmetic products. US rules require cosmetic facility registration and product listing, which is different from approval.

### What is a normal MOQ for bulk argan oil?

It varies by supplier and format. Bulk orders are often quoted per 5L, 25L or 200L unit, and private label per number of bottles. Ask the supplier for its current minimums for your exact product and packaging.

### Should I pay 100% upfront?

For a first order, staged payment is more common and protects both sides - for example a deposit, then the balance against the pre-shipment COA and documents.

### Can I visit the supplier in Morocco?

A serious producer should welcome a visit or offer a live video tour of the facility. Hesitation here is worth noting.
